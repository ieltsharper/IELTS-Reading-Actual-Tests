A Promise on a Piece of Paper

A For most of history, money was a thing of value in itself: a silver coin was worth what the silver in it was worth. Such money has one great disadvantage: it is heavy. A merchant who wished to buy goods at the other end of a kingdom had either to carry a chest of coin along roads where robbery was a serious risk, or to find some way of making a payment without moving metal at all. Paper money began as an answer to that practical difficulty, and only later became a currency in its own right. The crucial step was a change in what the object in the hand actually was. A coin is wealth; a note is a claim on wealth, a promise made by somebody else, and the whole history of paper money is the history of how far such promises can be trusted.

B The first such promises on paper that we know of were issued in Tang dynasty China, more than a thousand years ago. Traders bringing goods to the capital were reluctant to carry the proceeds home in copper coin, and arrangements grew up under which the coin was deposited with an agent in one city and a certificate issued which could be presented for coin in another. The documents were nicknamed flying cash, and they were not money in the full sense: they were not intended to circulate, and each one was a record of a particular deposit made by a particular person. What they did show was that a piece of paper could do the work of a cart-load of metal.

C Notes that genuinely passed from hand to hand appeared a little later, in the province of Sichuan, and the reason they appeared there is instructive. Copper was scarce in the region and the local coinage was made of iron, which is worth far less by weight. The result was a currency so bulky that a household buying a roll of cloth might have to move a load of metal heavier than the cloth itself. Merchant houses in the provincial capital began to accept deposits of coin and to issue printed receipts for them, and because everyone knew the houses that issued them these receipts were soon being handed on in payment rather than taken back for metal. In the early eleventh century the authorities, troubled by the failure of some of these private issuers, suppressed them and took the business over, printing notes themselves which were valid within a defined region and for a fixed period and which were supported by a reserve of coin. This is generally regarded as the first paper currency issued by a government anywhere.

D Under the Mongol emperors of the Yuan dynasty, paper money reached its medieval height. Notes printed on paper made from mulberry bark were issued across an empire that stretched far beyond China proper, and they were declared the only lawful means of payment: a merchant who refused to accept them was liable to severe punishment, and gold and silver were to be handed in to the treasury in exchange for paper. Travellers from Europe, where every payment still meant counting out metal, found the arrangement astonishing, and the Venetian Marco Polo devoted an admiring chapter of his account of his travels to the emperor who, as he saw it, turned bark into treasure.

E The weakness of the system was not technical but political. A government that can print its own money is a government that can meet a deficit without raising taxes, and successive Chinese dynasties did exactly that. Each new issue reduced the value of the notes already in circulation; prices rose, confidence fell, and people who could do so hoarded metal. By the fifteenth century the notes of the Ming dynasty had lost almost all their purchasing power, and the state eventually gave up the attempt. For several centuries afterwards China conducted its large transactions in silver, weighed out and assessed for purity at each payment — a cumbersome method, but one that no official could inflate with a printing block. The episode established the lesson that every later issuer has had to learn: paper money costs almost nothing to make, and that is precisely its danger.

F Europe arrived at the same institution by a different route. Merchants there had long used bills of exchange, written instructions by which a debt incurred in one city could be settled by a correspondent in another, and these too allowed trade to be financed without shipping coin. The decisive development came in seventeenth-century London, where goldsmiths, who possessed strong rooms, took in coin for safe keeping and gave depositors written receipts promising to return it on demand. Such receipts began to circulate, and the goldsmiths noticed that only a small proportion of the coin they held was ever called for at any one time, so that they could safely lend out more in receipts than they actually held in metal. Sweden, where the main coinage consisted of copper plates of extraordinary weight, was the first European country to issue true banknotes, in the seventeenth century; the bank that issued them printed more than it could redeem and failed within a few years. The Bank of England, founded in 1694 in order to lend money to a government at war, issued notes that promised to pay the bearer on demand, and that formula survives, long after the promise itself has changed its meaning.

G Over the following two centuries the right to issue notes was gradually concentrated in a single institution in each country, and the promise they carried was tied to a fixed quantity of gold. That tie proved to be a fair-weather arrangement. Convertibility was suspended in wartime, restored with difficulty, suspended again, and finally abandoned altogether in the early 1970s. What a modern note promises is therefore nothing more definite than itself. Its value rests on the restraint of the institution that issues it and on the fact that the state will accept it in payment of taxes and the courts will recognise it in settlement of debts. Cash is now in retreat, as payments move to entries transferred between accounts, but the principle being transferred is the one that was invented, lost and invented again over a thousand years.

Bước 3 – Đọc One-Shot: đến đoạn nào chốt hết câu hỏi của đoạn đó

Đoạn A – Trước đây tiền là vật có giá trị tự thân nhưng rất nặng, nên tiền giấy ra đời để khỏi phải chở kim loại; khác biệt mấu chốt: đồng xu là của cải, tờ giấy chỉ là lời hứa về của cải.

  • Lưu ý: Đoạn mở đầu nêu luận đề ("the history of how far such promises can be trusted") nhưng không chứa câu hỏi nào. Đọc nhanh, nắm ý rồi đi tiếp.

Đoạn B – Những lời hứa trên giấy đầu tiên được biết tới là ở đời Đường: thương nhân gửi tiền đồng cho đại lý ở thành này và nhận chứng thư đổi lấy tiền ở thành khác – gọi là "flying cash", chưa phải tiền thực thụ vì không nhằm lưu thông.

  • Câu 18 → B. "the first paper promises of this kind that are known to have been issued" ↔ "The first such promises on paper that we know of were issued" – gần như nguyên văn. Trích: "The first such promises on paper that we know of were issued in Tang dynasty China, more than a thousand years ago"
  • Lưu ý: Bẫy giữa Q18 và Q16: đoạn B là tờ giấy ĐẦU TIÊN, đoạn C mới là tờ giấy đầu tiên thật sự LƯU THÔNG. Hai câu hỏi khác nhau nên hai đoạn khác nhau.

Copper was scarce in the region and the local coinage was made of iron"

Đoạn D – Dưới các hoàng đế Mông Cổ nhà Nguyên, tiền giấy in trên giấy vỏ dâu được phát hành khắp đế quốc và được tuyên bố là phương tiện thanh toán hợp pháp duy nhất; ai từ chối nhận sẽ bị phạt nặng, còn vàng bạc phải nộp vào kho đổi lấy giấy.

  • Câu 14 → D. "a currency that people were not permitted by law to turn down" ↔ "the only lawful means of payment" + hình phạt cho người "refused to accept them". Trích: "they were declared the only lawful means of payment: a merchant who refused to accept them was liable to severe punishment"
  • Câu 21 → A. "a merchant who ___ to take it in payment risked a severe penalty" ↔ "refused to accept them"; chọn "refused" (A), còn "agreed" (H) là phương án trái nghĩa đặt sẵn để bẫy. Trích: "a merchant who refused to accept them was liable to severe punishment"

Đoạn G – Quyền phát hành dần tập trung vào một định chế mỗi nước và lời hứa được gắn với vàng, nhưng việc đổi ra vàng bị đình chỉ nhiều lần rồi bỏ hẳn đầu thập niên 1970; tờ tiền ngày nay chỉ hứa chính nó, giá trị dựa vào sự tiết chế của định chế phát hành và việc nhà nước, tòa án công nhận nó.
