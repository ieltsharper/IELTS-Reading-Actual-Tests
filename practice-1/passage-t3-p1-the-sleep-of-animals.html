The Sleep of Animals

Nothing that has been examined closely turns out to be entirely awake.

Sleep is awkward to define and easy to recognise. Rather than argue about what is happening inside the head, researchers use a short list of behavioural signs. A sleeping animal takes up a posture, and usually a place, that is typical of its species. It becomes much less responsive to the world around it, so that a sound or a movement which would ordinarily produce a reaction produces none. The state can be ended by a strong enough disturbance, which is what separates sleep from a coma or from anaesthesia. And if the animal is kept awake, it afterwards sleeps longer or more deeply than usual, an effect known as rebound. Measured against this list, sleep has been found not only in mammals and birds but in fish, in insects such as the fruit fly, and even in the jellyfish, which has no brain at all.

In mammals and birds, electrical recording divides sleep into two conditions so different that it is slightly misleading to give them a single name. In slow-wave sleep the cells of the cortex fire together in long, synchronised bursts, so that the brain produces large, slow waves; breathing and heart rate are steady and the body can move. In the second state, called rapid eye movement or REM sleep after the darting of the eyes beneath the closed lids, the pattern of brain activity is almost indistinguishable from waking, while the muscles of the body lose their tone so completely that the animal is briefly paralysed. It is from REM sleep that a disturbed sleeper wakes with the clearest sense of having dreamed.

The arrangement raises an obvious problem for an animal that must keep working while it sleeps. A dolphin is an air-breathing mammal in water, and it cannot afford to stop swimming or to stop rising to the surface. Dolphins meet this difficulty by sleeping with one half of the brain at a time: slow waves appear in one hemisphere while the other stays awake, and the eye on the opposite side of the body is closed while its partner remains open. After a period the two halves change over. Several birds can do the same. Where a row of ducks rests together on the ground, the birds at either end of the row tend to sleep with their outer eye open and the matching half of the brain awake, while those in the middle, screened by their neighbours, close both eyes.

A few birds appear to sleep while flying. Recorders small enough to be carried by a large seabird have captured the slow waves of sleep in frigatebirds during long crossings of open ocean, mostly in one hemisphere at a time and mostly while the bird was circling upwards on a rising current of warm air rather than flapping. The totals were small, a matter of minutes a day against many hours on land, which suggests that a bird in the air sleeps as little as it can manage. Swifts are thought to do something similar, since individuals of some species stay airborne for months on end between breeding seasons and cannot be sleeping on a branch.

Animals that are hunted face a different difficulty: a body that takes a long time to get up is a body that is easily caught. A horse can doze on its feet because an arrangement of tendons and ligaments in its legs locks the joints and holds its weight with almost no effort from the muscles. That trick will not serve for REM sleep, in which muscle tone is lost, so a horse must lie down for a short period each day if it is to have any REM sleep at all. Horses kept in a place where they never feel safe enough to lie down eventually begin to collapse from the lack of it.

How much sleep an animal needs varies enormously. A brown bat may spend the greater part of the twenty-four hours asleep. An elephant, recorded in the wild with a device fitted to its trunk, sleeps only about two hours a day, and will go without sleep altogether for a night or more while travelling. The sloth was long presented as the champion sleeper on the strength of measurements made in zoos; when sloths were fitted with recorders and followed in the forest, they proved to sleep several hours less than their captive relatives, a result that has made researchers cautious about every figure gathered in captivity. Across species the broad pattern is that small animals with fast metabolisms sleep more than large ones, and that predators sleep more than the animals they prey on, although the exceptions are numerous.

Why any of it happens is still argued over, and the arguments are not exclusive. The oldest proposal is that sleep saves energy, by shutting an animal down during the hours when it could not feed or hunt efficiently in any case. A second is that sleep is a form of safety, keeping an animal still and hidden when being active would expose it. A third concerns maintenance: fluid moves more freely through the narrow spaces of the brain during slow-wave sleep than during waking, which may help to carry away the waste products of the day. A fourth concerns learning. When an animal has spent a day finding its way through a maze, the patterns of activity recorded while it learned the route are replayed during the sleep that follows, and the connections involved are reinforced, so that memories formed during the day survive better than they otherwise would.

Whatever the purpose, it is plainly not optional. Rats prevented from sleeping for long enough lose control of their body temperature, eat more and more while losing weight, and die within a few weeks. Hibernation, incidentally, is not a long sleep and should not be confused with one: the body is cold, the brain is largely inactive, and animals rousing from it sleep heavily for days, exactly as though the weeks of cold had left them short of sleep.
