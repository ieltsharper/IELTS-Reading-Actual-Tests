Who Decides What the Machines Will Do?

A Arguments about machines and work follow a script so familiar that both parts can be recited in advance. One side announces that a new technology is about to make human labour unnecessary. The other replies, patiently, that this has been said at every stage of industrial history and has never come true: mechanisation destroyed particular occupations, but it also made goods cheaper, created demand that had not existed, and brought into being whole categories of work that nobody had been able to imagine. The quantity of work in a society is not a fixed lump to be divided up, and anyone who assumes that it is will reach gloomy conclusions by arithmetic alone. All of this is correct, and it is also beside the point, because it is an answer to a question that very few people are actually asking. The serious worry has never been that a day will arrive on which there is no work left to do. It is a worry about who loses, at what moment in their life, and on what terms.

B The machine-breakers of the early industrial period have become a byword for futile resistance to progress, which is unfair to them and unhelpful to us. They were not ignorant of machinery; many were highly skilled men whose trade had taken years to learn and could be acquired in weeks once a machine did the difficult part. Within their own working lives the value of that skill collapsed, and the wages that had supported a household fell to a level that would not. It is true that their grandchildren found employment in industries which could not have existed without the machines their grandfathers attacked. But a recovery that takes two generations is not a recovery for the generation that pays for it, and a benefit distributed across a whole society is no answer to a cost concentrated on a few thousand households in a single valley. Averages conceal precisely the thing that is at issue.

C Is the present wave genuinely unlike the earlier ones? Something can be said for the claim. Previous machines took over physical operations and, later, arithmetic; the systems now spreading operate on language, images and judgement, which widens the range of tasks exposed and means that the people affected include many who assumed their work was protected by education. Yet confidence about the pace of the change is not warranted by the record. Tasks that were declared to be months away have remained stubbornly undone for a decade, while others that were thought to be safely human gave way almost without warning. The honest position is that the direction of travel is reasonably clear and the speed of it is not, and arrangements built on a particular date are likely to fail. What follows is not paralysis but a preference for measures that work whether the change arrives in five years or in fifty.

D The most important question is the one that is least often framed as a question at all, which is where the gains go. When a process is automated, the value that used to be paid out in wages does not vanish; it is redistributed. It may go to the owners of the equipment as profit, to customers in the form of lower prices, or to the workers who remain, whose output per hour has risen. Which of these happens is not determined by the machine. It depends on how concentrated the industry is, on the bargaining strength of employees, on how the proceeds are taxed, on who owns the equipment, and on whether people displaced from one occupation can get the training that would let them enter another. Technology fixes what is possible; institutions decide the shares. A complaint about automation is therefore almost always a complaint about an arrangement, and arrangements, unlike physics, are open to amendment.

E If the loss were only a loss of income, the remedy would be straightforward, and the various proposals for paying people an income whether or not they are employed would settle the matter. The evidence from the study of unemployment suggests that it would not. People who lose work report the disappearance of things that money does not restore: a shape to the day, a reason to leave the house, contact with people who are not family, a position that can be named when strangers ask, and the sense of being depended upon by somebody. Their health and their contentment decline further than the fall in their income can explain, and those who find new work of a similar kind recover in a way that those who merely receive money do not. A payment addresses the part of the loss that is easiest to measure and the smallest part of what is actually gone.

F None of which should be turned into a defence of work as such, and here the argument often goes astray. A great deal of employment is monotonous, physically damaging or humiliating, and it is noticeable that the warmest tributes to the dignity of labour are composed by people whose own working days are varied, autonomous and comfortable. A machine that takes over the lifting in a warehouse, the inspection of a sewer or the handling of dangerous chemicals removes a genuine harm from somebody's life, and it is not progress to keep such jobs in existence on the grounds that they are jobs. The reasonable aim is fewer bad jobs rather than more of them, which means that the objection cannot be to automation in general. It must be to the particular way in which the costs and the benefits are currently allocated.

G Put the strands together and the ethical question comes into focus. It is not whether machines should do work that people now do; much of that transfer is desirable and all of it is coming. It is who decides which tasks are handed over, at what speed, which people are expected to absorb the disruption, and who collects what is saved. Those decisions are made by companies choosing what to invest in, by governments choosing what to tax, subsidise and teach, and occasionally by organised employees choosing what to accept. They are choices, and they could be made differently. The one response that is certainly wrong is to speak of automation as though it were the weather: something that happens to a society, about which the only sensible attitude is to carry an umbrella.
