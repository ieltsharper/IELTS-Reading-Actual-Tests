Two Languages in the Classroom

In every country that receives migrants, and in a great many that do not, children arrive at school speaking a language other than the one the school teaches in. What a school should do about this is among the most bitterly contested questions in education. Two intuitions pull in opposite directions. The first is that a child who needs to learn a language should hear and use as much of it as possible, and that time spent on anything else is time wasted. The second is that a child learns most readily through a language already understood, and that a school which ignores what a child brings is throwing away the only foundation available. Much of the heat in the argument comes from the fact that the phrase bilingual education is applied to arrangements whose aims have almost nothing in common.

It is worth separating them. In a transitional programme, the home language is used as a temporary bridge: children are taught the curriculum partly in it while they acquire the language of the school, and are moved into ordinary classes after two or three years, once they are judged able to cope with the language of instruction. The aim is competence in the majority language, and the home language is a means to that end. A maintenance programme, by contrast, goes on using the home language throughout, on the grounds that literacy in it is worth having for its own sake. A dual-language programme is different again: two groups of children, speakers of each of the two languages, are taught together, each group learning the other's language, with the intention that all of them leave school able to use both. Finally there is structured immersion, in which everything is taught in the majority language, but in a simplified and carefully supported form, the home language being used only occasionally, to explain something a child has not understood. The label on a programme therefore tells an observer very little about what happens inside it.

The case for teaching through the home language rests on a straightforward observation about transfer. A child who has learned to read in one language does not have to learn to read all over again in another. The understanding of what print is for, and the habit of predicting what a sentence will say, are not tied to a particular vocabulary. Subject knowledge behaves in the same way. Fractions learned in one language do not have to be learned again in another; only the words for them do. There is a further point about how long it takes to be ready to learn in a new language. Children pick up conversational fluency quickly, often within a year or two, and a child who chats easily in the playground is widely assumed to be ready for the rest of the timetable. The language of textbooks and examinations, however — abstract, dense, full of constructions nobody uses in speech — takes a good deal longer to master, and a pupil who is treated as fluent before reaching that point may sit through years of lessons understanding only a fraction of them.

The case against is equally serious. The simplest version of it concerns time: hours spent learning in the home language are hours not spent in the language the child will be examined in and will work in, and in a crowded timetable something has to give. Critics also point to the risk that children remain in separate classes long after they could manage in the mainstream, in a track which, being separate, attracts lower expectations. Then there are the practical obstacles. A single city school may contain children speaking dozens of languages, and no school can staff a programme for each. Even where one language dominates, teachers who are fluent and literate in it, trained in the curriculum and qualified to teach are often simply not available, and suitable books and materials may not exist. It is also a mistake to assume that migrant parents all want the home language in school. Many, anxious that their children should get on, ask for immersion and regard anything else as a distraction.

What does the research show? Less than either side claims, and for reasons that are built into the problem. Children are not allocated to programmes at random: families choose, and schools select, so that the pupils in one kind of programme may differ from those in another before any teaching has taken place. Programmes bearing the same name vary enormously in how they are staffed and run, and outcomes are measured at different ages and by different instruments. Reviews that pool the available studies tend to report a modest advantage for programmes that make substantial use of the home language, an advantage that is clearer on long-term measures than on tests taken soon after a programme begins. But the advantage is smaller than its advocates suggest, and the difference between a well-run and a badly run version of the same model is usually larger than the difference between one model and another.

One body of evidence deserves particular care, because it is so often misused. The French immersion programmes developed in Canada, in which English-speaking children are taught largely in French, have been studied for decades and are by any measure a success: the pupils become competent in French without falling behind in English or in other subjects. This is regularly cited as proof that immersion works and that teaching through the home language is unnecessary. The inference is unsound. Those children were learning an additional language while their first language remained the dominant and prestigious one in the society around them, supported by their families, the media and the law, and participation was voluntary. Adding a second language to a secure first language is a quite different undertaking from replacing a home language with the language of a majority that often regards the first as a handicap, and results from the one case cannot simply be carried across to the other.

It is worth noticing how such questions are actually decided. In one American state, voters passed a measure in the late 1990s that largely prohibited teaching in languages other than English in public schools, and nearly twenty years later voters in the same state reversed much of that restriction. The evidence available on the two occasions was not greatly different; the mood of the electorate was. Debates about language in school are rarely only about attainment: they are also about national belonging and about who is expected to adapt to whom, and such questions are not settled by test scores.

The useful question is therefore not which model wins, but under what conditions a given approach works: for how long the home language should carry part of the teaching, what training the teachers need, and how to judge the point at which a child is genuinely ready to learn in the new language. Almost everyone now agrees that a child's home language is an asset rather than an obstacle. What remains in dispute is how much of a school's limited time and money should be spent protecting it — a question about priorities, which no experiment can answer on its own.

Bẫy cần tránh

  • Nhóm 33–36 là "quà" của đề: cả bốn định nghĩa nằm gọn trong một đoạn, nên hãy đọc đoạn đó thật chậm và chốt ngay bốn câu, đừng để dành đến cuối giờ.
  • Người viết trình bày cả hai phía nhưng luôn kèm cụm đánh giá riêng ("The inference is unsound", "smaller than its advocates suggest", "The useful question is therefore not which model wins"). Với Yes/No/Not Given, chỉ những cụm này mới là quan điểm người viết; mong muốn của phụ huynh hay lời phe này phe kia thì không.
  • Cẩn thận với các câu hỏi gài sẵn một khuyến nghị ("should be given the final decision") hoặc một phép khái quát ("can reasonably be applied"): có đủ từ khóa trong bài không có nghĩa là người viết đã nói điều đó.
