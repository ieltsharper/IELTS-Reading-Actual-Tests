Two Wheels

The bicycle took eighty years to arrive at a shape it has barely altered since.

There is nothing in a bicycle that could not, in principle, have been made in the ancient world, and yet the machine is barely two hundred years old. The delay is usually explained by metal. A bicycle depends on parts that are light, accurately round and accurately matched — bearings, a chain, thin tubes, wire spokes under tension — and such parts only became cheap once industry could roll, draw and cut steel in quantity. The history of the bicycle is therefore less a story of a single inventor than a story of a series of workshops solving one problem at a time, each solution creating the next difficulty.

The ancestor of all of them appeared in Germany in 1817, when Karl von Drais built a wooden running machine: two wheels in line, a padded saddle on a beam between them, and a front wheel that could be steered. It had no pedals, and the rider propelled it by pushing against the ground with the feet, in long strides, lifting them when the machine ran downhill. It looks today like a toy, but it settled the question on which everything later depended, which is that a two-wheeled vehicle does not fall over while it is moving and can be balanced by steering. For two or three seasons it was fashionable in London and Paris. Then the fashion passed, not least because riders took to the pavements, where they frightened pedestrians and were promptly banned, and the roads of the day were too rough and too muddy to be enjoyable.

The next step waited half a century. In Paris in the 1860s a workshop fitted cranks and pedals directly to the axle of the front wheel, so that the rider could drive the machine without touching the ground. The result was called a velocipede, and in English, with feeling, the boneshaker. Its wheels were wooden with iron tyres, its frame had no springing to speak of, and the streets were cobbled; the name was earned. All the same it was the first bicycle to be manufactured in large numbers, it was exported across Europe and to America, and it produced the first racing clubs and the first road races. It also exposed a problem. Because the pedals turned the wheel directly, one turn of the rider's legs carried the machine forward by exactly the circumference of that wheel, and no faster.

The answer was to make the wheel enormous. Through the 1870s the driven front wheel grew until it was as large as the rider's legs could straddle, while the trailing wheel shrank to little more than a stabiliser, producing the high bicycle, later nicknamed the penny-farthing. These machines were a real advance in engineering. Their wheels were built on the modern principle, with thin wire spokes carrying the load in tension rather than thick wooden ones carrying it in compression, and they ran on solid rubber tyres, so the high bicycle was both faster and lighter than anything before it. It was also alarming. The rider sat almost directly above the axle of a wheel five feet across, with his weight high and well forward, and if that wheel struck a stone or a rut the machine stopped and he did not, but was pitched over the handlebars head first. Falls of this kind were common enough to have a name of their own. The high bicycle remained, inevitably, a machine for athletic young men with money.

What replaced it was the safety bicycle, which took shape in English workshops in the 1880s and reached its familiar form in Starley's Rover of 1885. Its central idea was indirect drive: a chain running from a pedalled sprocket to a smaller sprocket on the rear wheel, so that one turn of the pedals produced more than one turn of the wheel. Gearing had separated speed from wheel size. The wheels could now be equal, and modest, the rider could sit low between them with his feet within reach of the ground, and the frame settled into the familiar arrangement of triangles that gives the greatest stiffness for the least weight. The last piece arrived in 1888 with the pneumatic tyre, a tube of air inside a rubber casing, which made riding far more comfortable on rough surfaces and, to everyone's surprise, faster as well, because a tyre that absorbs a bump wastes less of the rider's effort than a rigid one that is thrown into the air by it. The idea had in fact been patented decades earlier and forgotten.

The 1890s were the result: a boom in which bicycles were bought in millions, and then, as the new machines passed into second-hand hands, a change in who could travel. A working man could reach employment ten miles from his village. A woman could leave her own street unaccompanied, which is why the bicycle appears so often in the arguments of the period about what women might wear and where they might go; the heavy skirts of the day were impossible on a machine, and the dress reformers made the point loudly. The industry mattered too. Firms that had learned to make ball bearings, light steel tubing, chains and wheels in quantity were precisely the firms that went on to build motor cars and, in more than one famous case, aircraft. Cyclists had meanwhile organised themselves and begun to campaign for smoother road surfaces, well before the motorist existed to benefit from them.

After about 1910 the bicycle lost its glamour in the wealthier countries and became, for fifty years, the transport of those who could not yet afford a car, while in much of the rest of the world it simply remained the way people got about. Its recent revival in European cities has less to do with nostalgia than with arithmetic: measured as energy spent per person per kilometre, a cyclist is the most efficient traveller on earth, more efficient than any machine and than any walking or swimming animal. It is a curious kind of success. Almost everything else built in 1890 has been superseded, but the bicycle in a modern shop is recognisably the safety bicycle, improved in its materials and barely altered in its plan.

Đoạn B – Tổ tiên của xe đạp là máy chạy bộ bằng gỗ của Karl von Drais (Đức, 1817), không bàn đạp, người lái đẩy chân xuống đất, và nó chứng minh xe hai bánh không đổ khi đang chạy.

Đoạn C – Thập niên 1860 ở Paris, bàn đạp được gắn thẳng vào trục bánh trước tạo ra velocipede ("boneshaker"): bánh gỗ vành sắt, xóc kinh khủng, nhưng là xe đạp đầu tiên sản xuất hàng loạt và bộc lộ hạn chế về tốc độ.

Đoạn D – Thập niên 1870, bánh trước được phóng to hết cỡ chân người lái tạo ra high bicycle (penny-farthing): nan thép chịu kéo, lốp cao su đặc, nhanh và nhẹ hơn nhưng rất nguy hiểm vì người lái bị văng qua ghi đông.

  • Câu 18 → TRUE. "diameter ... limited by the length of the rider's legs" ↔ "as large as the rider's legs could straddle" (to hết mức chân còn dang tới) → TRUE. Trích: "the driven front wheel grew until it was as large as the rider's legs could straddle"

Câu dài: xương sống là "the machine stopped and he did not"; mệnh đề điều kiện "if that wheel struck a stone or a rut" nêu nguyên nhân, mệnh đề "but was pitched over the handlebars" nêu hậu quả.   • Câu 25 → chain. "A ___ running back to a smaller sprocket" ↔ "a chain running from a pedalled sprocket to a smaller sprocket"; chỉ một từ → chain. Trích: "a chain running from a pedalled sprocket to a smaller sprocket on the rear wheel"
