The Unseen Journey of the Eel

For as long as people have fished the rivers of Europe they have caught eels, and for most of that time nobody could say where an eel came from. Fishermen took them from ponds, ditches and estuaries, and found that the animals they cut open contained no eggs and no recognisable reproductive organs at all. Eels seemed to appear in waters that had no connection with any other, including isolated pools, and they could be seen on wet nights crossing damp grass from one ditch to the next. Aristotle, who examined the question as carefully as anyone in the ancient world, concluded that eels were not born but formed spontaneously out of mud. Variations on that idea survived for centuries, and the puzzle of the eel’s origin was still unsolved when biology had explained the life cycle of almost every other European fish.

The answer, when it came, was assembled from the sea rather than from the river. Early in the twentieth century the Danish scientist Johannes Schmidt began a long series of Atlantic voyages, towing fine nets at depth and collecting the small, leaf-shaped larvae that were by then known to be young eels. His method was simple in principle: wherever he sailed, he measured the larvae he caught, reasoning that the smallest ones must be the newest and so would be found nearest to the place where they had hatched. Year after year the catches led him west and south, and the tiniest specimens of all came from the Sargasso Sea, a warm, still region of the western Atlantic bounded not by coasts but by circling currents. Schmidt concluded that the European eel spawns there and nowhere else. A century later this remains the accepted account, although no adult European eel has ever been observed spawning in the wild, and the eggs themselves have never been collected from the open ocean.

The creature that hatches is so unlike an eel that it was described as a separate species before anyone realised what it was. A leptocephalus is flattened from side to side, shaped like a willow leaf and almost perfectly transparent, with a body of jelly-like tissue and a thread of gut. It is a feeble swimmer, and for the crossing of the Atlantic it is carried rather than carrying itself, riding the broad drift of warm water that runs north-east towards Europe. The journey takes a year or more, and the larva feeds on fine organic material suspended in the surface water rather than hunting prey.

Arrival at the edge of the continental shelf triggers the first of several transformations. The leaf-shaped larva shrinks, thickens and becomes a glass eel: a transparent miniature of the adult, still without pigment, so clear that the spine can be seen through the flesh. Glass eels gather in estuaries in winter and spring, and use the rising tide to carry themselves inland, holding position near the bottom while the water falls. As they take on colour they are known as elvers, and they begin to push upstream, climbing wet stone and weed around obstacles that would stop most fish.

There follows the long, obscure middle of the eel’s life. In rivers, lakes, marshes and estuaries the animal becomes a yellow eel, so called for the dull olive and brass of its flanks, and it may remain in this stage for anything from a few years to several decades, hunting at night, lying under stones by day and growing very slowly in cold water. One of the stranger features of the species belongs to this period. An eel’s sex is not fixed when the egg is fertilised; it is settled much later, and appears to depend on the conditions in which the young animal grows, with crowded waters producing a different balance of males and females from sparsely populated ones.

At last something prompts the eel to leave. Over a few months its body is rebuilt for a journey it will make only once. The eyes enlarge greatly and their pigments shift towards the blue of deep water; the pectoral fins broaden; the back darkens and the flanks turn a metallic silver that makes the animal hard to see from above or below. The gut degenerates and the eel stops feeding altogether, so that the entire crossing must be paid for out of stored fat. These silver eels drop downstream on dark, rainy autumn nights and disappear into the Atlantic, and almost nothing is known of what happens next, beyond the fact that they are thought to die after spawning.

The European eel is now in serious trouble. The number of glass eels reaching European estuaries each spring is a small fraction of what was recorded a few decades ago, and the species is classed as critically endangered. No single cause explains the decline. Weirs, sluices, dams and hydroelectric turbines stand between the sea and much of the habitat the eel once used, blocking young eels on the way up and killing silver eels on the way down. A parasitic worm that lodges in the swim bladder, brought to Europe with eels imported from East Asia, is now widespread and may leave infected animals unable to complete a long swim at depth. Pollution leaves chemical residues in the fat the eel depends on for its final journey. Glass eels are fished intensively, in part because eel farming has never managed to breed the animal through every stage of its life and so remains dependent on young eels caught in the wild. Shifts in the ocean currents that deliver the larvae may matter as much as any of these, and are the hardest of all to manage. An animal that was once so common that it was given to the poor is now the subject of urgent conservation effort across a continent.
