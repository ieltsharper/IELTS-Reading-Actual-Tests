Setting the Clocks

How the railway and the telegraph persuaded the world to abandon its own local hour.

A For almost all of recorded history, time was a local matter. Noon was the moment at which the sun stood highest over a particular town, and since the sun reaches different places at different moments, every town kept an hour of its own. Bristol was roughly ten minutes behind London; a traveller moving west across England was, in effect, moving slowly backwards through the morning. Nobody was inconvenienced by this. A journey by coach took so long, and was so uncertain, that a discrepancy of a few minutes between one church clock and the next was invisible. Appointments were made for the morning or the afternoon rather than for the half-hour, and the working day was measured by daylight and by the bell.

B The railway destroyed that comfortable vagueness within a generation. A train could cross the distance between two towns faster than the difference between their clocks, which produced timetables that appeared to describe arrivals before departures. More seriously, much early track was single, with trains passing one another at agreed places and agreed moments; if the two drivers were working to clocks set in different towns, the agreement was worthless. Passengers missed connections, and inspectors investigating collisions found that they could not establish the order in which events had happened. Speed had turned a harmless irregularity into a hazard, and the companies, rather than any government, were the first to act on it.

C What made a shared hour practical was not the railway but the electric telegraph, which carried a signal from one end of a country to the other in a moment. Time could now be sent as well as kept. The Royal Observatory at Greenwich had long dropped a large ball down a mast at one o’clock each afternoon so that ships in the river could check their chronometers before sailing, but a time ball can only be seen from a few miles away. Once observatories were wired to the telegraph network they could distribute a signal daily to stations, post offices and jewellers along the line, and the jewellers in turn corrected the watches of the town. An accurate hour had become something that could be transmitted, bought and sold.

D Through the 1840s the British railway companies adopted a single standard, Greenwich time, across their networks, and by the middle of the century most public clocks in the country followed them. The change was not universally welcomed. To set a clock by an observatory two hundred miles away was to accept that a distant institution now defined when a local day began and ended, and some towns kept both hours going at once, with a second minute hand painted on the dial of the town clock. There was also a legal muddle, because the law still recognised local time: a document signed, a licence expiring or a poll closing at a stated hour could fall on either side of a deadline depending on which clock was consulted. Parliament did not confirm Greenwich time as the legal time of Great Britain until 1880, forty years after the railways had settled the matter in practice.

E North America faced the same problem on a far larger scale. Its railroads ran on dozens of different company times, and a large interchange station might display half a dozen clocks, one for each line using the platforms. In 1883 the railroads themselves agreed to divide the continent into a small number of broad belts, within each of which every clock would show the same hour, with a jump of exactly sixty minutes at each boundary. Again the industry moved first: the change was introduced by private agreement, and the government of the United States did not give the arrangement the force of law for another thirty-five years.

F A continent could organise itself, but a world could not do so without agreeing where to begin counting. Charts and almanacs measured longitude from a variety of starting lines, and a system of hourly zones is meaningless unless everyone uses the same one. The Canadian railway engineer Sandford Fleming argued through the 1870s and 1880s for a single global scheme of standard hours reckoned from one meridian. At a conference held in Washington in 1884, delegates from a large number of countries recommended that the meridian passing through Greenwich should be adopted as the common zero, a decision that owed less to astronomy than to the fact that most of the world’s shipping already carried British charts. The recommendation bound nobody, and adoption took decades; several countries kept their own meridian for years afterwards, and a few regions chose offsets of half an hour rather than a whole one, as some still do.

G The result was a quiet revolution in everyday discipline. Once an authoritative hour was available to everyone, it could be used to govern not only trains but shifts, school lessons, market openings and the working day itself, and lateness became a measurable offence rather than a matter of opinion. The modern descendants of the time ball are the signals that synchronise computer networks and satellites, accurate now to fractions of a second, but the principle is unchanged: somewhere a clock is defined as correct, and everybody else agrees to follow it. It is worth remembering that the hour shown on a phone is not a fact about the sun. It is an administrative convenience, invented for the convenience of traffic, and the sun over most towns is no longer overhead at noon.
