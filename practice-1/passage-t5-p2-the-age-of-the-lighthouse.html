The Age of the Lighthouse

A A coastline is not a line but a problem. Approached from the sea at night, a low shore is invisible, a headland looks like an island, and the reef that will open a ship's hull lies a mile from anything that can be seen at all. A light placed on the shore solves two difficulties at once. It warns that a danger is near, and, if the mariner knows which light he is looking at, it also tells him where he is, which is the harder and more valuable service of the two. For most of recorded history there was no reliable way of fixing a position out of sight of land, so the sight of a known light after a long passage was the moment at which guesswork ended. Societies that depended on sea trade therefore found ways of paying for lights, usually by charging a due on every cargo landed, long before they would pay for roads of comparable usefulness.

B The earliest towers were simply high places on which a fire could be kept burning. The most celebrated of them, the Pharos of Alexandria, was raised in the third century BC, was counted among the seven wonders of the ancient world, and stood for well over a thousand years before earthquakes brought it down. The Romans built lesser towers at the mouths of their harbours, including one that still survives within the walls of Dover Castle in England. In medieval Europe the work often fell to religious houses, and a chapel on a cliff with a lamp in its window might be maintained by monks who were also paid to pray for sailors. The fuel was wood and, later and more usually, coal, burned in an open iron basket at the top of the tower. Such a fire was genuinely bright, but it consumed an enormous weight of fuel, every ton of which had to be carried up by hand, and it burned worst in precisely the conditions in which it was most needed, since rain drowned it and a gale either blew it out or smothered it in its own smoke.

C The hardest lights to establish were those on rocks that the sea covered at high water, and the long argument over one of them, the Eddystone reef off Plymouth, became the school in which the problem was solved. The first tower there, an elaborate wooden structure finished in 1698, was swept away in a great storm five years later, taking its builder with it. The second, completed in 1709 and built largely of wood on a stone base, lasted almost fifty years before it burned down. The third was the decisive one. Finished in 1759, it was built of stone throughout, and its designer solved the problem of a structure that the waves would try to lift rather than merely push: each block was dovetailed into its neighbours and into the course below, so that the tower behaved as a single mass, and it was bedded in a lime that would set under water instead of a mortar that would wash away. Its profile, broad at the base and curving inwards as it rose, was deliberately modelled on the trunk of an oak, a shape chosen because a tree survives the wind that fells a wall. Every wave-swept tower built since has followed the same principles.

D Solving the building left the light itself, which until the end of the eighteenth century was poor. The improvement came from two directions. The first was a new oil lamp with a hollow circular wick and a glass chimney, which drew air through the middle of the flame as well as around it and so burned far more steadily and brightly than a wick in a dish. The second was optical. A flame radiates in every direction, and a light at sea needs almost all of that radiation sent outwards in a narrow horizontal band. Curved metal reflectors placed behind the lamp achieved part of this, but they absorbed a good deal of the light they handled. The answer, introduced in the 1820s, was a lens assembled from concentric rings of glass, each ring ground to the angle required to bend the light passing through it into the beam. Because the thickness of a conventional lens had been replaced by a series of thin steps, the new apparatus was lighter, cheaper and far less absorbent than a solid lens of the same power would have been, and it increased the range of a light out of all recognition. The lamps beneath it changed repeatedly over the following century, from vegetable and mineral oils to vaporised paraffin, gas and finally electricity, but the lens did not need to change at all.

E A powerful light raised a new difficulty: on a coast with many lights, a brilliant one is useless if the mariner cannot tell which it is. Each station was therefore given its own pattern of flashes and intervals of darkness, and these patterns were printed in the lists of lights that every navigator carried, so that a sailor could identify a station by counting with his watch. Producing the pattern meant turning the whole lens assembly around the lamp at a precise speed. Early arrangements ran on rollers and wore out quickly; the elegant solution was to float the entire rotating structure, which might weigh several tonnes, in a shallow bath of mercury, where it could be set moving with the pressure of one hand. The motive power was clockwork, wound up by the keeper at intervals through the night, with a weight descending slowly down the centre of the tower exactly as in a longcase clock.

F All of this required keepers, usually three at an isolated station so that two were always available and no man was ever alone with a body if one of them died. Their work was unromantic. They trimmed wicks, polished glass, wound the clockwork, kept a written log and painted whatever was within reach, and in bad weather they waited, sometimes for weeks beyond the date on which a relief was due. Automation ended the profession in the course of the twentieth century. Once lamps could be changed by a mechanism, failures reported by radio and the whole installation supervised from an office on shore, there was no case for keeping men on a rock, and the last keepers were withdrawn from most national services before the century ended. Satellite navigation has since removed the second of the lighthouse's two functions altogether, and a modern ship knows its position to within a few metres without looking out of the window. Lights are nevertheless still maintained, because electronics fail and the rocks do not move, and the towers themselves have acquired a new value as monuments to a period when the only way to make a coast safe was to put a man on it with a lamp.

Đoạn D – Sau khi giải xong phần xây dựng là đến bản thân ánh sáng: đèn dầu bấc tròn rỗng với ống khói thủy tinh cháy sáng và ổn định hơn, và thấu kính ghép từ các vòng kính đồng tâm (thập niên 1820) hướng ánh sáng thành chùm ngang.

  • Câu 17 → vi. Hai hướng cải tiến = ngọn lửa tốt hơn (đèn bấc rỗng) + cách hướng ánh sáng tốt hơn (thấu kính vòng) ↔ heading vi "Better flames and a better way of aiming them". Trích: "The improvement came from two directions"
