Room to Breathe: Green Space and the Health of Cities

A That parks are good for us has become one of those propositions that nobody in urban policy troubles to argue for any more. It appears in planning documents, in the prospectuses of property developers and in the speeches of mayors, generally in a form broad enough to be unfalsifiable. The proposition is, in the main, true; what is rarely acknowledged is that it covers several quite different claims, supported by evidence of very unequal quality, and that the differences between them have consequences for how a city spends a limited budget. A claim that trees reduce the temperature of a street in a heatwave is of a different order from a claim that a park improves the mental health of the people who live near it, and both differ from a claim that planting one will make a neighbourhood more equal.

B The starting point is a very large body of population research, gathered in many countries, which consistently finds that people living in greener neighbourhoods report better mental health, show lower levels of stress hormones and suffer less cardiovascular disease than people living in less green ones, and that these differences survive statistical adjustment for income and education. The consistency is impressive. The difficulty is that almost all of this work is observational, and observational data cannot establish which way the causation runs. Greener districts tend also to be quieter, cleaner and less crowded, and no adjustment captures every one of those advantages. There is a further and more awkward possibility, which is that people who are already inclined to walk, to garden and to spend time outdoors may be more likely to choose a green neighbourhood in the first place, so that part of the correlation reflects who moves there rather than what living there does to them.

C This is why a handful of studies with stronger designs carry weight out of proportion to their number. Several research teams have followed the same individuals through a change of address, comparing their reported wellbeing before and after a move to a greener area, and have found an improvement that is still present some years later, together with a corresponding decline among those moving the other way. A second approach has been to treat the greening of derelict land as an experiment: vacant plots in a city are cleared, planted and fenced, while similar plots nearby are left untouched, and the surrounding streets are then compared. Neither method is a clinical trial, but both remove the most obvious objection to the population studies, since the people are the same people, and both point in the same direction as the weaker evidence.

D How a patch of grass and trees produces such effects is a separate question, and the candidate explanations are neither mutually exclusive nor equally well supported. The first is physical activity: green space is held to encourage walking and play. The evidence here is more mixed than one might expect, since much of the activity recorded is done by people who would have exercised somewhere in any case. The second is straightforwardly physical. Vegetation intercepts particles, muffles traffic noise, and shades and cools the ground beneath it, and in a heatwave the difference of a few degrees between a shaded street and a bare one is a matter of survival for the old and the ill; of all the mechanisms proposed, this is the best established. A third is social: a shared and pleasant outdoor space brings neighbours into casual contact, and casual contact is the raw material of the loose acquaintance that protects people against isolation. The fourth is psychological. Concentrated attention of the kind that city streets and screens demand is tiring, whereas a natural scene holds the attention without effort and so allows the tired faculty to recover; the experimental support for this is modest in size but has proved durable. The practical lesson is that these benefits can be separated. A strip of planting along a motorway that nobody can enter delivers the cooling and the filtering and nothing else.

E It follows that the measure by which provision is almost always reported — the total area of green space per thousand residents — is close to useless. Area says nothing about whether the space is reachable, usable or used. Distance is the first and harshest filter: the proportion of residents who visit a park regularly falls away sharply once it is further than a few minutes' walk from the door, so that a dozen small squares will be used where one great lawn on the edge of the district will not. After distance come the things that determine whether a visit is repeated: whether the place is well maintained rather than neglected, whether it feels safe, particularly to women and to the elderly, and whether it offers shade, seating and drinking water. Variety of planting appears to matter as well; the available work suggests that a mixture of trees, shrubs and rough grass does more for wellbeing, as it does for wildlife, than the same area of mown turf. None of these qualities appears in a hectare count, and a city that manages its parks by that number will build the wrong ones.

F There is also the question of who gets them. Green space is distributed across almost every city in the same pattern: least of it in the poorest and most densely built districts, which are also the hottest, the noisiest and the most exposed to traffic fumes, and whose residents have the least opportunity to leave the city at weekends. Those with most to gain have least to use. The obvious remedy, which is to plant where provision is worst, runs into a difficulty that was not anticipated when the first such programmes began. A neighbourhood made more attractive becomes more expensive; rents and prices rise around a new park, and some of the households the improvement was intended to serve are in time displaced by households who can pay more, a process that has been given the sardonic name of green gentrification. The answer to this is certainly not to withhold trees from poor streets. It is that a greening programme not accompanied by protection for the tenants living there will, in the end, deliver its benefits to somebody else.

G What, then, is an honest summary? Access to green space appears to produce a small improvement in the health and the contentment of a great many people, at a cost that is low and an effect that lasts for as long as the space is maintained. That profile — modest benefit, very wide reach, durable, cheap — is precisely the profile of the public health measures that have historically done the most good, and it is also, unfortunately, the profile that is easiest to oversell. When a park is presented as a substitute for mental health provision, or when street trees are offered as a serious answer to a city's carbon emissions, the claim goes well beyond anything the research will bear, and each such claim makes the defensible ones easier to dismiss. The useful conclusion is an unglamorous one. A park is not an ornament added when the building is finished, nor a cure for the failures of everything else. It is infrastructure, and like the drains and the lighting it should be planned where people actually live, budgeted for, and maintained in the years after the ribbon has been cut.
