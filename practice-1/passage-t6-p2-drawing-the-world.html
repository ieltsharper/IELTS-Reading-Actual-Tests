Drawing the World

Every map is an argument about what matters, and the history of map-making is a history of changing purposes.

A map is not a picture of the world. It is a flat, reduced and selective statement about the world, and each of those three words conceals a problem that map-makers have spent several thousand years trying to solve. Flatness requires a method for representing a curved surface on a sheet. Reduction requires a consistent scale. Selection requires somebody to decide what is worth drawing, and that decision has always depended less on the land itself than on what the map was for.

The oldest surviving attempt at a map of the whole world is a small Babylonian clay tablet. It shows the known lands as a disc, with Babylon near the middle and a ring of water, labelled as a bitter river, drawn around the outside. Beyond that ring the tablet places regions described in writing rather than drawn to any scale. The object is not a guide to travel; it is a statement about the shape of creation and about where its centre lies, and in that respect it set a pattern that would be followed for a very long time.

Greek scholarship introduced measurement. Having accepted that the earth was a sphere, Greek geographers set about estimating its size, and the best known of these attempts used the difference between the shadows cast at noon on the same day in two Egyptian cities a known distance apart. The result was remarkably close to the modern figure. The tradition reached its height in Alexandria in the second century AD with Ptolemy, whose Geography was less a map than a set of instructions for making one. It listed thousands of places as pairs of numbers, a latitude and a longitude, so that a map could be reconstructed from the text alone by anyone who understood the system, and it discussed how the curved surface might be laid out on a flat page. Ptolemy also made the earth considerably too small, an error that was still being repeated more than a thousand years later and that encouraged navigators to believe Asia lay within an easy westward sail.

Ptolemy’s work was preserved and extended in the Arabic-speaking world while it was largely unknown in western Europe. Scholars there corrected his coordinates where travellers’ reports allowed; the most celebrated of their own surveys was compiled in twelfth-century Sicily for a Norman king, combining the accounts of merchants and pilgrims into a map and an accompanying description of the inhabited world. Its orientation, with south at the top, is a useful reminder that north is a convention and not a fact.

In medieval Europe the dominant form was the mappa mundi, which organised the world around a sacred centre rather than around a journey. East was placed at the top, Jerusalem at the middle, and the space between was filled with cities, rivers, scenes from scripture and the strange peoples and beasts that were believed to live at the edges of the earth. Such maps are often described as inaccurate, but accuracy of position was not what they were attempting. They were summaries of knowledge and belief, meant to be read and contemplated, and a traveller would no more have used one to find a road than a modern reader would navigate by a diagram of the solar system.

Practical navigation produced a quite different object. From the thirteenth century onwards, Mediterranean sailors used portolan charts, whose coastlines were built up from the accumulated records of voyages and are startlingly close to their true shapes. These charts show almost nothing of the interior, but they are covered by a web of straight lines radiating from compass roses placed around the sheet, allowing a navigator with a compass to lay off a course between two ports. Here, for the first time, the map was a working instrument, and its makers were tradesmen serving shipowners rather than scholars serving princes.

Ocean voyaging made the problem of flatness urgent. On a globe, a course steered at a constant compass bearing is a curve, and on an ordinary chart it cannot be drawn as a straight line. The projection published by Gerardus Mercator in the sixteenth century solved this by stretching the spacing of the parallels towards the poles by exactly the amount needed to make such a course appear straight. The price was a severe distortion of area: on a Mercator map the lands of the far north and south swell enormously, so that Greenland rivals Africa, which is in truth many times larger. The projection is therefore excellent for the single purpose for which it was designed and misleading for almost every other, which is the ordinary condition of maps.

Position at sea remained half-solved. Latitude could be found readily enough by measuring the sun or a star above the horizon, but longitude requires comparing local time with the time at a chosen reference meridian, and no clock would keep time through months of damp, rolling weather. The eventual answer, in the eighteenth century, was a marine timekeeper built to run accurately at sea. On land the same century saw governments begin systematic national surveys based on triangulation, in which a single baseline is measured on the ground with great care and a chain of triangles is then extended across the country from it by observing angles alone. These surveys were slow and costly, and they were paid for by states with military interests rather than by the sellers of maps.

Photography from the air, and later from orbit, changed the gathering of information rather than its purpose. A modern mapping agency works from imagery that is refreshed continually, and the position of any point can be fixed by satellite to within a few centimetres. Yet the final questions are the ones the Babylonian scribe faced: what to include, what to leave out, what to place at the centre and whose names to use. A digital map chooses on the user’s behalf, quietly and invisibly, and is no less a selection for being made of data.
