Building for a Warmer World

A Of all the things human beings make, buildings are among the longest-lived and the most demanding. They consume a large share of the energy used in the world, both in keeping their occupants warm, cool and lit and in the manufacture of the materials from which they are assembled. What makes this more than an engineering problem is the timescale. A car is replaced within a decade or two; an office block may still be standing in a hundred years, and decisions taken on a drawing board in a single afternoon — how deep the building is, which way its windows face, how its structure is framed — will govern its energy consumption for the whole of that life. Sustainable design is therefore less a style than an attempt to take seriously the fact that such choices cannot easily be revisited.

B It is worth remembering that for most of history builders had no choice but to pay attention to climate, because they had no machinery with which to overcome it. In hot, dry regions the answer was mass: walls of stone or mud brick thick enough to absorb the heat of the day and release it slowly through the cold night, small openings, pale surfaces, and rooms arranged around a shaded courtyard. Some towns added tall shafts that caught the prevailing wind and drew it down into the rooms below. In hot, wet regions, where the nights bring little relief and mass is useless, the opposite logic applied: light construction, floors raised off the damp ground, wide overhanging eaves, and walls designed to let air pass straight through. These were not quaint local manners but solutions tested over many generations, and they were largely abandoned once cheap electricity and mechanical air-conditioning made it possible to put up a sealed glass tower in Stockholm and an almost identical one in Singapore and keep both at the same temperature by force.

C Much of what is now called sustainable design is the recovery of that older intelligence, expressed in modern terms. Orientation comes first: in a cool climate the long face of a building is usually turned towards the midday sun to gather warmth, and in a hot one away from it. Shading is then sized to the geometry of the sun, since a horizontal projection that excludes high summer sunlight will admit the low winter sun beneath it. A building shallow enough in plan can be lit by daylight and ventilated by opening windows on opposite sides, or by allowing warm air to escape through a tall space and draw cooler air in behind it. None of these measures involves expensive technology, and all of them cost very little if they are settled while the plan is still being drawn. Once the frame is up, almost none of them can be added without rebuilding.

D Northern Europe has pursued a different and more technical strategy, in which the building is treated as a vessel that must not leak. The envelope is wrapped in a continuous layer of insulation, junctions are detailed to avoid the paths through which heat escapes at corners and around openings, the glazing is triple, and the structure is sealed so thoroughly that its airtightness is tested before completion. A house built in this way may need almost no heating even in a hard winter. The approach has two consequences that its enthusiasts have sometimes been slow to admit. A building that admits no air by accident must be given air deliberately, so mechanical ventilation, with a device that recovers heat from the outgoing stream, becomes a necessity rather than a luxury. More seriously, a vessel designed to retain heat retains it in July as well as in January, and as summers grow hotter such buildings can become uncomfortable or even dangerous unless they are properly shaded and can be flushed with cool air at night.

E As the energy used in running buildings falls, attention has shifted to the energy and emissions locked up in their construction. Cement is the difficulty. Making it requires heating limestone to a high temperature, and the chemical reaction that follows releases carbon dioxide from the rock itself, quite apart from whatever fuel is burned to provide the heat, so that concrete carries a heavy cost before a building is even occupied. Several responses are being pursued: replacing part of the cement with industrial by-products, designing structures that use less material, reclaiming steel sections from demolition for reuse, and building in timber, which holds carbon absorbed while the tree was growing. The most effective response of all is the least glamorous. Keeping an existing building and improving it avoids nearly all of these emissions, and a structure that is demolished and replaced by an efficient new one may take many decades to repay, in saved running energy, the carbon spent on its own construction.

F A further and uncomfortable discovery is that buildings designed to be efficient frequently are not. Where consumption has been measured after occupation, it has often turned out to be substantially higher than the figures produced during design. The reasons are mundane: insulation installed with gaps, ventilation systems left in the wrong mode because nobody explained them to the caretaker, controls that occupants override because they are confusing, and modelling assumptions about hours of use that bear little relation to the event. Certification schemes have not always helped, because most of them award credit for features specified on paper rather than for consumption recorded in use, and a building can therefore collect an impressive rating and still perform poorly. The remedies are unexciting: commissioning the systems properly, explaining them to the people who will operate them, and publishing measured consumption year after year.

G Finally, the frame of the question is widening. A house that uses little energy but stands where every journey must be made by car may be responsible for more emissions than a less efficient flat in a place where daily life can be managed on foot, so location and density belong in the calculation. So does adaptability. Buildings that survive for centuries usually do so because generous floor heights and simple plans allow them to become something else when their first purpose expires, while a structure tailored precisely to one tenant tends to be demolished when that tenant leaves. The conclusion is that sustainability in building is less a matter of the devices fixed to a roof than of unexciting decisions about where to build, what to build it from, how to shape it and whether it can be changed.
