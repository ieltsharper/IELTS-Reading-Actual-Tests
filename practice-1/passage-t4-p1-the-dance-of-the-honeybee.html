The Dance of the Honeybee

How a colony of insects tells its members where to find the next meal.

A honeybee colony may contain tens of thousands of workers, and in the course of a summer day it has to find and exploit patches of flowers that are scattered over many square kilometres of countryside and that may come into bloom and fade within a week. No individual bee can survey such an area, and there is nobody in the hive to issue orders. What the colony has instead is a system of communication in which a forager that has found something worth collecting is able to tell her nestmates where it is. The system was worked out in the first half of the twentieth century by the Austrian zoologist Karl von Frisch, whose patient experiments with marked bees and dishes of sugar water eventually earned him a share of a Nobel Prize.

A forager that has returned from a rewarding patch does not settle down quietly. She climbs on to the vertical face of a comb, pushes into the crowd of workers there and begins to dance. If the flowers are close to the hive, the dance is a simple one: the bee runs in a tight circle, reverses, circles again, and goes on reversing for several seconds or more. This round dance says nothing about direction. It announces only that something worth having lies somewhere in the immediate neighbourhood, and the bees that follow it leave the hive and search in all directions around it. They are not, however, searching blindly, because the scent of the flowers she has visited still clings to her body, and a sample of the nectar itself is passed from her mouth to theirs.

When the food is further off, the forager performs a quite different movement, the waggle dance. She runs forward in a straight line, shaking her abdomen rapidly from side to side and producing a buzzing vibration as she goes, then loops back to her starting point, to one side on one circuit and to the other side on the next, so that her path traces a flattened figure of eight. The straight portion, known as the waggle run, carries the message, and two of its features matter. The first is its angle. The comb hangs vertically in the dark, so the bee uses gravity in place of the sky: a run made straight up the comb means that the food lies in the direction of the sun, while a run thirty degrees to the left of vertical means that the food lies thirty degrees to the left of the sun. The second feature is duration. The longer the waggle run lasts, the further the forager has flown, so that distance is expressed as time rather than as length. A single dance may repeat the run dozens of times, and since it is performed in the darkness of the hive the bees that follow cannot see it at all; they press close behind the dancer, touch her with their antennae and pick up the vibrations she sends through the comb.

A direction given relative to the sun has an obvious weakness, which is that the sun moves. Foragers solve this by keeping track of the passage of time, and a dancer who is detained inside the hive will slowly alter the angle of her run, as though following a sun she cannot see. Cloud is a smaller problem than it appears. A patch of blue sky is enough, because a bee can detect the pattern of polarised light that the sky produces, and that pattern reveals where the sun must be.

The dance reports more than a position. The richer the source — the sweeter the nectar, the easier the flowers are to work, the shorter the flight — the more vigorous and the more persistent the dance, while a mediocre find produces a brief dance or none at all. Since the number of recruits that leave depends on how long and how energetically the dancer performs, the number of foragers visiting a site rises quickly while it remains productive and falls away when it does not. No bee compares the patches available to the colony, and none needs to.

For many years this account was not universally accepted. A number of researchers argued that recruits in fact found the food by smell, following the odour of the flowers and of the foraging bees themselves, and that the dance, however elegant, told them nothing they could use. The objection was a fair one, since in ordinary circumstances the two explanations predict much the same behaviour. Two kinds of experiment eventually settled the question. In the first, a mechanical model of a bee was made to perform waggle runs inside a hive, and recruits flew out to the place the model had indicated, although no bee had ever been there. In the second, individual recruits were fitted with tiny transponders and tracked by radar as they left the hive; they flew the course that the dance had specified, and then began to search when they reached the end of it.

Other signals are exchanged on the same crowded dance floor. A forager who comes back loaded with nectar and cannot quickly find a house bee to take it from her will perform a tremble dance, walking slowly about with her legs shaking, and the effect of this is to recruit more workers to the business of receiving and storing nectar rather than to the flowers. There is also a short vibrating pulse, delivered by one bee butting its head against a dancer, which makes the dancer break off; it is given by bees that have met with danger at the site being advertised, and it damps down recruitment to a dangerous place. The same waggle dance is used for a quite different purpose when a colony divides and swarms. Scouts go out to look for a cavity in which the swarm can live, and each returns to dance on the surface of the clustered bees for the site she has found, the persistence of her dance reflecting how good she judges it to be. Scouts are drawn away to inspect the sites that rivals are advertising, and dancing for the poorer cavities gradually ceases, until almost every dancer is advertising the same address and the swarm flies to it.
